THE MOON IS SUS

source: dude, trust me

A browser RPG about a man who notices the pigeon on the power line hasn’t moved in four hours. The title is a joke. The premise is a joke. The codex is not.

The mechanic01
Lore 50 — forgeries called out on sight
Contact
What the file says
Four feet tall, lit from inside, and it spoke into your head without sound. Two taller ones behind it searched the undergrowth and never once looked at you. All three stopped existing at the same instant.
What you can prove
Nothing. Not one thing. No photograph, no witness, no recording, no mark. You have just had the most vivid experience of your life and you cannot hand a single part of it to another human being.
The Green Glass MemoPlanted
What the file says
A leaked memo proves the municipal water supply carries a compliance agent, and the treatment plant logs prove it.
What you can prove
The memo has a letterhead that did not exist in the year it is dated. Whoever made it did not check. Somebody wanted you to spend a week on this.

Every conspiracy you uncover is filed with two columns. The left is what the theory claims. The right is what can actually be shown — and it stays honest however supernatural things get, including when the thing it is being honest about is you.

Why the early game is confusing on purpose02

Your Lore level decides what you can read

The evidence column is gated on a skill. At Lore 1 every entry reads “you have no idea how to check this.” At 10 you get a hint. At 25 the full evidence. At 50 the forgeries are flagged on sight.

Which makes the theme into a mechanic: levelling Lore literally makes you better at spotting a planted lead, in step with the character getting better at it. A first-time player at Lore 3 will chase the forged memo. That is correct. That is the lesson, and no tooltip could teach it.

Most of it is true. Some of it is planted.

  • Chasing a planted lead costs you time, Heat and a contact. It is never a game over — being wrong is the subject, not a failure state.
  • Whether an entry is a forgery is never sent to the browser below Lore 50. A conspiracy game you can inspect-element the answer out of is not a conspiracy game.
  • Heat, not sanity. Every game in this genre reaches for a sanity meter and it ages badly — it equates investigating with becoming mentally ill. Heat does the same job without the baggage, and unlike sanity it can be spent.
What’s in it03

The eye

Watch the bird through binoculars. The first two looks are nothing — that is what makes the third one land. The eye extends, focuses, retracts. Then the head turns.

It has to eat the bread

A surveillance drone that ignores free bread in front of a human has blown its cover. It comes down because it must, and it eats on a perfect one-second metronome, and it never once looks up.

the tell is the animation

Examine everything

Every lamppost, bin and bird has a description, and reading one pays Lore. Looking at things properly is literally how you get better at not being fooled.

A clock that skips

The first thing that goes wrong is not in the world — it is the clock in the corner of your own screen. The game never announces it. If you weren’t looking, you miss it.

no monster required

It says the line twice

“I am not here to hurt you. I am here to help you.” Then again, identically — same words, same gaps, same weight on the same syllable. Not a reassurance. A recording.

Two men in a car

Lying and telling the truth are both wrong. Everything about the encounter is legal, deniable and unremarkable, and that is the part that should frighten you.

Skill-gated dialogue

Options you can’t afford are shown greyed with what they need. A door you can see and can’t open teaches you the system exists; a hidden door teaches nothing.

Combat is deliberately the worst way to level

Encounters are scripted and finite. Nothing ever respawns. Combat exists so danger is real — it is not the loop.

No audio files

Every sound is synthesised at runtime — oscillators and filtered noise. When the encounter starts, the ambient bed cuts out entirely. After ten minutes of hum, silence is the loudest thing available.

Where it goes04
The spine

MERIDIAN 12 does not hide the truth. It publishes it — buried in enough manufactured nonsense that anyone who finds the real thing sounds exactly like someone who found a fake one. Discrediting a truth is cheaper than suppressing it, and it scales.

Which means the final boss is not a monster. It is an editor. And the birds are not the conspiracy — they are the audit trail, watching which threads you pull so the next planted lead can be aimed at you properly.

And it is a prequel

Twelve chapters end with you doing something with MERIDIAN 12’s archive, and MERIDIAN 12 responding the way it always does with people who found out: not killing you. Relocating you.

Which solves the oldest problem in the genre. Nobody has to explain why a shared world contains ten thousand chosen ones: every player down there finished this game. They are all there for the same reason.

Where I’m honest about it

Two of twelve chapters exist. Roughly twenty minutes of play. The art is placeholder on purpose — if a beat works with rectangles it will work with art, and if it needs art to work it was never a beat.

Nothing here depicts real living people, and real trademarked places have been renamed. The ranch is Hollow Mesa and it is nowhere. The real history the game leans on — Operation Highjump, the Stargate Project, AAWSAP — is documented, and the codex is careful to say which parts are and which parts aren’t.

The title is a joke. The codex is not.

A game about conspiracy theories that will tell you when the conspiracy is nonsense — including when the nonsense is the one you just spent an hour chasing.
